What to say when they pick up

Five things, in this order+
  1. Where you are. Street, landmark, floor, flat number. Location first, in case the call drops.
  2. What happened. One sentence.
  3. How many people are hurt, and how badly.
  4. Whether anyone is trapped, unconscious, or not breathing.
  5. Your name and this phone number.
  • Stay on the line until they tell you to hang up. They may talk you through first aid.
  • If the call won't connect, send an SMS — texts often get through when calls don't.
Numbers verified against Indian government listings. Some are state-specific and a few districts still route differently — 112 is the one that works everywhere. Add your own local control room under My info.

Call 112 first if you can, then start. If someone else is with you, one person calls while the other begins.

Not breathing — CPR (adult)+
  1. Shout at them and shake their shoulders. No response and no normal breathing? Start now.
  2. Call 112 and put the phone on speaker beside you.
  3. Lay them flat on their back on a hard surface. Kneel beside their chest.
  4. Heel of one hand on the centre of the chest, other hand on top, fingers interlocked.
  5. Push straight down hard — about 5 to 6 cm — and let the chest come all the way back up.
  6. Keep a rate of 100 to 120 pushes a minute. Don't stop.
  7. Only stop when they wake up, help arrives, or you physically cannot continue. Swap with someone every 2 minutes if you can.
Gasping is not breathing. Occasional noisy gasps after collapse are a sign to start CPR, not a sign to wait.
Heavy bleeding+
  1. Press hard directly on the wound with a cloth, shirt, towel — anything.
  2. Keep pressing. Don't lift it to check. Add more cloth on top if it soaks through.
  3. Raise the limb above the level of the heart if nothing is broken.
  4. Once bleeding slows, bandage firmly over the padding.
  5. Lay them down, keep them warm, watch for shock.
Never pull out a knife, glass shard or rod stuck in the body — it may be plugging the bleed. Pad around it and leave it in place.
Choking+
  1. Can they cough or speak? Let them cough — don't interfere.
  2. Silent, clutching the throat, going blue? Act now.
  3. Lean them forward. Give 5 sharp blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
  4. Still stuck? Stand behind, fist just above the navel, grab it with your other hand, pull sharply inward and upward. 5 times.
  5. Alternate 5 back blows and 5 thrusts until it clears.
  6. If they go unconscious, lower them down and start CPR.
  • For a baby under one: 5 back blows face-down along your forearm, then 5 chest thrusts with two fingers. Never abdominal thrusts on an infant.
Unconscious but breathing+
  1. Kneel beside them. Place the near arm out at a right angle.
  2. Bring the far arm across the chest, back of their hand against their cheek.
  3. Pull the far knee up, foot flat on the ground.
  4. Roll them towards you onto their side by pulling that knee.
  5. Tilt the head back slightly so the airway stays open and any vomit drains out.
  6. Check they're still breathing every minute until help arrives.
If you suspect a neck or spine injury, don't roll them unless they're vomiting or choking. Support the head and wait.
Burns+
  1. Stop the burning. Move away from the source, smother flames.
  2. Cool under cool running water for a full 20 minutes. Start even if it's been a while.
  3. Remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the burn before it swells.
  4. Cover loosely with cling film laid lengthways, or a clean non-fluffy cloth.
  5. Keep the rest of the body warm — long cooling causes chills.
No ice, toothpaste, butter, ghee, haldi or ointment. Don't burst blisters. Don't peel off clothing stuck to the skin.
  • Go to hospital for burns to the face, hands, feet, joints or genitals, anything bigger than the person's palm, or any burn on a child.
Broken bones+
  1. Keep them still. Don't let them walk on it or test it.
  2. Support the limb in the position you found it, using your hands or rolled cloth.
  3. Pad around it and tie a splint — a stick, rolled newspaper, cardboard — above and below the break, not over it.
  4. For an arm, a sling made from a dupatta or shirt works well.
  5. Ice wrapped in cloth over the area helps with swelling.
Never try to straighten a bent limb or push a bone back in.
Shock+
  • Signs: pale clammy skin, fast weak pulse, shallow fast breathing, confusion, thirst, nausea.
  1. Lay them down and raise their legs about 30 cm if no leg or spine injury.
  2. Loosen tight clothing at the neck and waist.
  3. Cover them with a blanket or coat.
  4. Reassure them and keep checking breathing.
Nothing to eat or drink, even if they beg for water — they may need surgery.
Snakebite+
  1. Move away from the snake. Don't try to catch or kill it — photograph it from a distance only if safe.
  2. Keep the person absolutely still and calm. Movement spreads venom.
  3. Immobilise the bitten limb with a splint and keep it below heart level.
  4. Remove rings, bangles and anklets before swelling starts.
  5. Get to a hospital with antivenom immediately. Carry them; don't let them walk.
No tourniquet, no cutting, no sucking, no ice, no herbal paste. These cause more harm than the bite. Snakebites rise sharply during floods.
Pulled from water+
  1. Get them out without going in yourself if possible — reach with a stick, throw a rope or floating object.
  2. Once out, check for breathing.
  3. Not breathing? Start CPR straight away. With drowning, give rescue breaths first if you're trained.
  4. Breathing? Recovery position, remove wet clothes, wrap them warm.
  5. Take them to hospital even if they seem fine. Water in the lungs can cause problems hours later.
Don't hold them upside down or press the stomach to force water out.
Electric shock+
  1. Cut the power at the mains before touching them. This comes first, always.
  2. If you can't, push them clear with something dry and non-conducting — wooden stick, plastic chair. Never your hands.
  3. Stay well back from fallen high-voltage lines and call 112. Assume every downed wire is live.
  4. Once safe, check breathing. Start CPR if needed.
  5. Treat burns at the entry and exit points.
Heat stroke+
  • Signs: hot dry skin, temperature above 40°C, confusion, headache, no sweating, collapse.
  1. Move them into shade or a cool room immediately.
  2. Remove outer clothing.
  3. Cool fast — wet cloths, fan them, pour water over them, ice packs at the neck, armpits and groin.
  4. If conscious and alert, give cool water or ORS in sips.
  5. Keep cooling until help comes. Heat stroke is a medical emergency.
These are standard first-aid steps, not medical advice, and they're no substitute for hands-on training. Do a St John Ambulance or Red Cross course if you can — practising CPR once on a dummy is worth more than reading it ten times. When in doubt, call 112 and follow what the operator tells you over what's written here.

This app can't receive live alerts without a connection. For real warnings: install NDMA's Sachet app, keep cell broadcast alerts switched on in your phone settings, and keep a battery radio for All India Radio bulletins.

Flood+
  1. Move to higher ground early. Take the stairs, not the lift.
  2. Switch off electricity and gas at the mains before water reaches them.
  3. Never walk or drive through moving water. Thirty centimetres can sweep a car away; fifteen can knock you off your feet.
  4. If you must wade, use a stick to test the ground ahead for open drains and manholes.
  5. Drink only boiled or bottled water afterwards. Watch for snakes in debris.
Earthquake+
  1. Drop to your hands and knees before the shaking drops you.
  2. Cover your head and neck. Get under a sturdy table if there's one within a step or two.
  3. Hold on to it and move with it until the shaking stops.
  4. Indoors, stay indoors. Outdoors, move away from buildings, walls and power lines and stay put.
  5. After it stops, expect aftershocks. Check for gas leaks before using any flame or switch.
Don't run outside while it's shaking, and don't stand in a doorway — that advice is outdated for modern buildings.
Fire+
  1. Get everyone out. Don't collect belongings.
  2. Stay low — the breathable air is near the floor.
  3. Feel doors with the back of your hand before opening. Hot? Find another way.
  4. Never use the lift.
  5. Trapped? Shut the door, block gaps with wet cloth, go to a window and signal. Call 101 and tell them exactly which floor and room.
  6. Clothes on fire: stop, drop, roll.
Cyclone+
  1. Before: charge everything, fill water containers, secure loose objects outside, know your nearest shelter.
  2. During: stay indoors in the room furthest from windows. Keep away from glass.
  3. If the wind suddenly stops, you may be in the eye. Stay put — it will return from the opposite direction.
  4. After: watch for fallen live wires, damaged buildings and contaminated water.
Building collapse+
  1. If trapped, cover your mouth and nose with cloth against dust.
  2. Tap steadily on a pipe or wall so rescuers can hear you. Save your voice.
  3. Shout only as a last resort — it makes you inhale dust.
  4. Don't light a match or lighter; there may be gas.
  5. Avoid moving around or kicking up dust.
Go-bag: what to keep ready+
  • Drinking water, three litres per person
  • Dry food that needs no cooking
  • Torch and spare batteries, plus a power bank
  • Battery or wind-up radio
  • First-aid kit and a week of any regular medicines
  • Photocopies of Aadhaar, ration card and insurance in a sealed plastic bag
  • Cash in small notes — card machines fail when power does
  • Whistle, rope, sturdy shoes, spare clothes
  • Water purification tablets or bleach
